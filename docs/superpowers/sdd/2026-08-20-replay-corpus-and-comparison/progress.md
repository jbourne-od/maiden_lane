# Replay corpus and comparison — progress

Programme plan: docs/superpowers/plans/2026-08-20-replay-corpus-and-comparison.md

DECOMPOSITION FINDING that made this programme tractable, and it came from reading clauses 6 and 7 apart rather than as one thing. Clause 6 is a COMPARABILITY PRECONDITION: did the two sides run over the same corpus, under the same profile, in the same world, with an explicit correspondence between the checkpoint semantics compared. Clause 7 is the OUTCOME: no protected metric regression. They are usually thought of together as "did the change make things worse", and separating them means this programme can deliver clause 6 without inventing a metric system. A comparison whose comparability cannot be established is worthless whatever the metrics show, which is why it is first.
DECISION 1: a corpus is CONTENT-ADDRESSED, not a named collection. The reason is clause 6's own wording — it requires the two sides to have run over THE SAME replay corpus. Under a mutable name, sameness would be a claim about a label: two sides could satisfy the clause having run over different sets, and every comparison ever made against that name would retroactively be about something else. Content-addressing also gives the right failure, since adding a case changes CorpusID and a comparison over the enlarged corpus is visibly a different comparison rather than the old one with better numbers.
This DIFFERS from the target-policy decision in the previous programme, and the difference is in the specification rather than in taste. §14.1 says a publication record pins the policy VERSION, explicitly choosing a version over a derived identity. §14.2 says the corpus participates in a derived IDENTITY. Both calls were made by the spec; this programme only follows them.
DECISION 2, settled by a fact rather than a preference: InputID = H(StateDigest, WorldID), and §14.2 lists WorldID and CorpusID as SEPARATE inputs to Compare. Both point the same way, so a corpus is a set of case STATES and the world is pinned once for the whole comparison. CorpusID = H(ordered StateDigests). Together corpus and world determine every case's InputID, which is what makes "the same corpus under the same historical world" checkable rather than two labels that happen to match. §14.2's own phrase, "historical world/corpus", is one concept and this is what it decomposes into.
DECISION 3: comparison is a derived semantic artifact of the same kind as an assessment. §6 enumerates every derived identity and neither CorpusID nor ComparisonID appears — but unlike the target-policy case that absence is NOT decisive here, because §14.2 says in as many words that the inputs "participate in comparison identity". The spec asserts the identity exists; §6 simply does not enumerate it. The shape follows AssessmentID = H(CheckpointArtifactID, ProfileID), the existing precedent for a derived judgment about artifacts.
The two sides of a comparison are CheckpointIDs, not CheckpointArtifactIDs, and that is the crux. CheckpointID = H(PlanID, CheckpointKey) identifies a checkpoint DECLARATION — a piece of semantics — while CheckpointArtifactID identifies one realized checkpoint of one run. A comparison over a corpus has no single artifact per side; it has one per case. So what is compared is the semantics evaluated across the corpus, which is exactly §14.2's phrase "corresponding checkpoint semantics".
DECISION 4: correspondence is DECLARED, never inferred. Inference by name would be right most of the time, and the times it was wrong would be indistinguishable from the times it was right — two plans may legitimately name the same semantics differently and, far worse, may name different semantics the same.
COST STATED RATHER THAN DISCOVERED: a corpus of n cases needs n executions per side. It is affordable only because execution is deterministic and identity derived, so a case already executed under a given ExecutionID never needs executing again — and since the rehydration slice, the stored result is verifiably reproducible. A corpus costs n executions once; comparisons over it afterwards cost lookups.

Slice 1, corpus identity in the kernel: complete. This is the programme's first kernel change and it follows World exactly — validate, canonically order, refuse duplicates, domain-tagged versioned encoding, encoders and no decoders.
Corpus holds States rather than state digests. Accepting digests would let a CorpusID be derived over content nobody has; every digest here is one the kernel computed from a state it validated. It also makes the value useful, since running a side over a corpus needs the states and a corpus of hashes could only be compared, never replayed.
An empty corpus is REFUSED, unlike an empty world, and the asymmetry is deliberate: an empty world is a real statement — nothing was pinned — while a comparison over no cases establishes nothing about a candidate. Admitting one would let clause 6 be satisfied by being vacuously true, which is the most dangerous way for a gate to pass. A test asserts both halves so the asymmetry is anchored rather than described.
A duplicate case is refused rather than deduplicated. Silently collapsing it would answer a different question than the one asked while looking like it answered this one: a comparison reported over twelve cases that ran eleven is a false statement about coverage.
Case is indexed rather than returning the whole set, because copying every state to reach one would make iterating a corpus quadratic in its own size — a real concern at the hundreds of cases a corpus is expected to hold, and not one World has.
InputIdentities is asserted against BindRun's own derivation rather than against a recomputation of the same formula in the test. A corpus that derived inputs no execution could match would be useless while looking correct.
MUTATION TESTING, and the first attempt at it was worthless: removing the ordering and removing the duplicate check both produced BUILD failures from unused variables, so the tests never ran and proved nothing. Redone with mutations that compile — a no-op stable sort, and a condition short-circuited while keeping the loop index used — and all three properties then failed by name: order independence, duplicate refusal, and input-identity agreement. Worth recording as a method note: a mutation that does not compile is not a mutation test.

Slice 2, the comparison contract: complete. ComparisonPolicy with an explicit checkpoint correspondence, ComparisonPolicyID, and ComparisonID over §14.2's full formula.
Correspondence is authored as checkpoint KEYS and derived into identities against the two plans. Keys are the authoring form — somebody writing a correspondence knows that team_hos_aggregated.v1 is now team_hos_reconciled.v2 — and deriving against the plans means a pair naming a checkpoint neither plan declares cannot become a policy. A correspondence therefore cannot outlive the declarations it describes.
The one-to-one requirement in BOTH directions is the fail-closed property that matters most after declaration itself. One baseline mapping to two candidates, or two baselines to one candidate, makes "corresponding" ambiguous, and a comparison cannot fail closed on an ambiguity it silently resolved by taking the first match.
Corresponds checks BOTH sides rather than resolving the baseline and trusting the result. Checking only that the baseline maps to something would accept a comparison whose candidate side is a checkpoint the contract never mentioned. Mutation-tested: weakening it to a one-sided lookup is caught.
Failing closed happens at CONSTRUCTION, not at evaluation. NewComparison refuses when the policy declares no correspondence between its two sides, so an unidentifiable comparison cannot be handed to downstream code that might not remember to check. Checking later would make the refusal a behaviour somebody has to remember rather than a state that cannot be built.
The owner's forward constraint is honoured and documented on the type: a Comparison names the QUESTION. Per-case ExecutionIDs and CheckpointArtifactIDs are evidence and are deliberately absent from the identity, because folding them in would collapse question and evidence and make the identity change every time the comparison was re-evidenced — so the same question asked twice would be two questions. A test asserts the same request produces the same identity.
Checkpoint identities are asserted against SEAL's own derivation rather than a recomputation of the same formula, the same pattern as slice 1's InputIdentities-versus-BindRun check. A policy mapping checkpoints no sealed artifact ever names would otherwise look correct.
MUTATION TESTING FOUND A TEST THAT PASSED FOR THE WRONG REASON, which is the second time this method has earned its place in this programme. Five properties were mutated; four were caught. The escape: dropping the correspondence digests from the policy's canonical encoding, keeping only the count, was NOT detected — because the identity test varied the policy by supplying a NARROWER one, which differs in count. So the test proved the count participates, not the content.
The consequence would have been exactly the failure this identity exists to prevent: two policies over the same plans, with the same number of rows and different pairings, sharing one identity — letting a comparison be reinterpreted under a correspondence nobody declared. Closed with a test using two policies that are both valid, both one-to-one, both two rows, and mean completely different things, and verified by re-running the mutation.
METHOD NOTE, now twice-learned in this programme: a passing test is not evidence that the property it names is enforced. In slice 1 the failure mode was mutations that did not compile; here it was a fixture that varied the wrong dimension. Both were invisible from a green run.

Slice 3, corpus persistence: complete. ports.CorpusStore, both adapters, one contract suite, and a migration. store-check 79 to 97.
A corpus stores like a PLAN, not like a policy, and the plan document's word "append-only" was imprecise. Identity is content, so a corpus cannot be edited at all: PutCorpus is idempotent on CorpusID and there is no version, no update, and no delete. A corpus that should have held different cases IS a different corpus, and the one already stored must remain readable because comparisons pin its identity — deleting it would leave them naming a set of cases nothing can reconstruct.
KERNEL REFINEMENT FOUND BY WORKING THIS SLICE: a corpus must share one schema across every case. Storage wanted to keep the schema once, and the reason it can is stronger than convenience — BindRun refuses a state whose schema digest is not the plan's, so a mixed-schema corpus could never be replayed under ANY plan. It is unusable for its only purpose, and refusing it at assembly means that is discovered when the corpus is built rather than partway through executing it. Corpus.SchemaDigest() is exposed, and comparability will need it: both plans under comparison must pin that digest or the corpus is not replayable under both.
The schema is deliberately NOT part of CorpusID. Every case's StateDigest already commits to it, so encoding it would be a second statement of one fact.
Storage follows the ratified rehydration pattern rather than inventing one: a Corpus cannot be serialized, so the adapter stores the cases as documents, rebuilds them through the kernel's constructors on read, and REQUIRES the re-derived CorpusID to equal the one the row is filed under. That is the same rule plan storage follows when it recompiles on read, and it is what makes a stored corpus verifiable rather than merely well formed.
The projected columns — schema_digest and case_count — are checked against the rebuilt corpus rather than trusted. They exist so a reader can size work without decoding, and a column that disagreed with the cases would be a second description of them able to mislead.
The corruption test's most valuable case is a document that decodes cleanly, rebuilds into a perfectly valid corpus, and is a DIFFERENT corpus. Nothing about that row looks wrong; only the re-derived identity catches it.
FINDING WHILE WRITING THAT TEST: the "syntactically unreadable document" case is unrepresentable in this table, because the column is jsonb and PostgreSQL rejects malformed JSON on write — the UPDATE itself failed. The plan and execution tables store bytea and do test it. That difference is correct rather than an inconsistency: the column type is doing work here that application code has to do there. Replaced with a document that is valid JSON and describes no corpus, which the empty-corpus refusal then rejects, and the reason is recorded in the test so nobody adds the impossible case back.

Slice 4, running a side over a corpus: complete. RunCorpus and CorpusProgress in internal/app. NO new port, NO new table, NO new identity.
THE DESIGN DECISION: a side run has nothing of its own. A corpus, a plan, a world, an executor, and a provenance policy determine every case's ExecutionID through BindRun, so progress is answered by re-deriving those identities and looking them up rather than by reading a record somebody remembered to update. Nothing can drift from what it describes if nothing is stored. This is the same reasoning that keeps the gate's verdict out of the publication record, applied to orchestration state.
PLAN DOCUMENT CORRECTED, the second imprecision found by implementing the slice it describes: it said "enqueueing every case for one plan and profile". The profile is not an input to running. The spine assesses every sealed checkpoint under every profile its plan compiled, so a profile is a READ-SIDE selector used when comparability picks which assessment to read.
FAIL FAST ON THE SCHEMA, and this is where slice 3's kernel refinement pays off. A corpus whose cases are not under the plan's schema is refused before anything is enqueued, because BindRun rejects a state whose schema digest is not the plan's — so without the check the failure would arrive once per case, AFTER n durable executions had been created for work that cannot succeed. Mutation-tested: moving the check inside the loop is caught.
THE COST MODEL IS ASSERTED, NOT CLAIMED. CaseRun.Enqueued() reports whether THIS call created the execution, so a caller sees what a run actually cost rather than inferring it. Re-running a side enqueues nothing new, because ExecutionID is derived from the semantic request and a repeat is necessarily the same execution — not because anything remembers having run it. Mutation-tested: reporting every case as newly enqueued is caught by name.
CorpusProgress is a separate function rather than a flag on RunCorpus. "Tell me where this stands" and "start this" are different intents, and a boolean at a call site does not say which is meant. A test asserts a progress read reaches the queue not at all, which the counts alone would not prove.
Complete() is all-or-nothing by design: a comparison over a corpus where some cases have not run is a comparison over a different, smaller corpus, and reporting partial completeness would invite exactly that substitution. A terminally FAILED case counts as finished, because a computation that ran and refused produced a real answer — the same rule the rest of the system follows.
No cap on corpus size and no batching. A cap would silently run a different, smaller corpus than the caller named, and a corpus's identity is precisely the set of cases it holds. The programme's "no silent caps" constraint applies directly.
Absent corpus and absent plan get distinct codes, because an operator has to know which of the two names was wrong; a single not-found would send them to check both.

Slice 5, comparability and clause 6: complete. The gate answers SEVEN of nine and still refuses. Scoped to the pure evaluation, as the plan says; assembling evidence from stored executions is the application half and follows.
KERNEL ADDITION, one verifier: VerifyCorpusIdentity(digests, expected). It lets a PURE evaluation establish "the same replay corpus" without holding the corpus, which is exactly what content-addressing was chosen for in slice 1. A caller supplying the case digests it actually ran cannot make them add up to a corpus it did not run. Same one-way verifier shape as VerifyInvariantResultDigest: no constructor, no decoder.
It deliberately does NOT canonicalize. The digests must already be in the corpus's canonical order, so a caller cannot pass an arbitrary set and have it silently sorted into agreement — a set with the right members in the wrong order is a caller that does not know what it ran. An empty list verifies nothing, for the same reason the empty corpus is refused.
A BUG I NEARLY SHIPPED AS A CAVEAT, and writing the comment honestly is what exposed it. Coverage was collected from artifact.StateDigest(), which is the state at the CHECKPOINT BOUNDARY — after the transitions before it committed — while a corpus is a set of INITIAL states. The two coincide only for a checkpoint following no committed transition, so it would have verified for a first checkpoint and failed for every later one: correct in the simplest test and silently broken in use. The artifact carries InitialStateDigest, which is the right field. Mutation-tested both ways.
The lesson is worth keeping beside the mutation-testing ones: I was writing a comment explaining what a check does NOT establish, and the honest version of that sentence turned out to describe a defect rather than a limitation. A caveat that has to be that specific is usually a bug wearing a disguise.
THE OWNER'S FORWARD CONSTRAINT IS ENFORCED WHERE IT SAID: the world is checked on each ARTIFACT, never taken from whatever produced the evidence. A side run reporting the right world is a projection, and only the sealed artifact carries the world its execution actually pinned. A test re-identifies a comparison under a different world with the artifacts unchanged and requires Fail.
The clause distinguishes MISSING from WRONG throughout, which is the same discipline the readiness clause follows: no comparison, or a side with no evidence, is unevaluated; evidence that contradicts the comparison is a definite adverse finding. Six contradictions are tested — a comparison of another checkpoint, coverage that is not this corpus, an artifact realizing another declaration, an assessment bound to another checkpoint, an assessment under another profile, and a world the evidence did not run under.
Candidate.Comparison is a POINTER. Absence is meaningful and common — most candidates carry no comparison — and a zero-valued struct would make "no comparison" and "an empty comparison" the same thing, when the clause reports the first as unevaluated and the second as adverse.
CLAUSE 6 CHANGED KIND, not just verdict, and both the app and HTTP tests had to be updated to say so. It was UnsupportedByBuild; it is now InformationAbsent for a candidate carrying no comparison. That is precisely the progression recorded during the #17 review — UnsupportedByBuild, then InformationAbsent once implemented but unevidenced, then Pass or Fail with evidence — and it changes what an operator should do: supply something rather than wait for engineering. The wire test now asserts 6 pass, 2 unsupported, 1 absent rather than 6 and 3.
The fixture compares two checkpoint DECLARATIONS OF ONE PLAN, which the correspondence contract permits and which is the smallest shape exercising every check. A realistic comparison is across two plans over many cases; that needs both sides actually executed and belongs to the application slice that assembles evidence from stored executions. Recorded so the fixture's shape is not mistaken for the intended use.

Slice 5b, comparison evidence assembly: complete. AssembleComparison rehydrates both sides and produces evidence that satisfies clause 6. Everything before this made comparability DECIDABLE; nothing could decide it, because a comparison's evidence is authenticated kernel artifacts and those cannot be read out of a store.
THE COST IS PAID HERE AND IT IS THE ONE THE PLAN STATES: 2n deterministic re-executions for a corpus of n cases. Documented on the function, with the reason it is not a missing optimization — kernel values cannot be rebuilt from bytes, and recomputing an identity from stored components establishes only that a stored tuple agrees with itself. A caller wanting to know whether the work is DONE asks CorpusProgress, which costs lookups; a side reporting complete is a reason to attempt assembly, never a substitute for it.
DESIGN: the sides supply only executor identity and provenance policy. The PLANS come from the comparison's own correspondence, which pins both. Reading them from there rather than accepting them removes the possibility of assembling evidence from a plan the correspondence was never authored for — a mistake no later check would catch, because every artifact would be individually valid.
A GAP FOUND WHILE WIRING: the comparison pins WorldID, but binding a case needs the World VALUE, and a WorldID is one-way. So the world is supplied and VERIFIED against Comparison.World() rather than trusted — the same shape as the invariant witness. Without the check a caller could bind under a different world and derive executions the comparison does not name.
NO PARTIAL EVIDENCE. OWNER REVIEW of #34, and it is the review that found the operation was right and its BOUNDARY was missing. Reattempt gave an unattempted execution back to the queue; it did not give the store any way to tell which attempt an outcome belonged to. Proved with a throwaway probe before fixing: claim, abandon, reattempt, re-claim, then a stale Fail from the first attempt returned nil, terminated the third attempt's generation, and recorded the first attempt's plan_absent for a run that never happened in it. Classic ABA — the store checked that the execution was running, which it was, just not for the caller asking.
THE HLD HAD ALREADY NAMED THE ANSWER. §17 says a worker records its AttemptID and a retry receives a new one only after the prior lease has ended or expired. AttemptID was in the identity model as the one OPERATIONAL identity, ratified long before this store existed, and I built the reattempt operation without it. Worth recording: when an operation needs to distinguish two occurrences of the same work, check whether the identity model already has the identity for it.
Claim now mints the generation and returns it with the request; Complete and Fail require it. Postgres: WHERE execution_id = ? AND current_attempt_id = ? AND status = 'running'.
THE STATUS TERM IS LOAD-BEARING AND I NEARLY SHIPPED IT AS DECORATION. With status IN ('pending','running'), generation 0 is a UNIVERSAL KEY: an enqueued execution starts at 0, so any caller that never claimed anything could present the zero attempt and record an outcome for work nobody ran. Requiring 'running' closes it, because only a claim produces that status. Two existing contract tests had to start claiming first, and one assertion changed from expecting pending to running — which is the tell that the guard is real rather than redundant.
That change also exposed a hole in my own coverage: mutating the status term away killed NOTHING. Every test exercised claimed executions; none exercised the unclaimed one the term exists for. Now covered by name, over attempts 0, 1 and 7, for both Complete and Fail, asserting the execution is still pending and still resultless afterwards. Generalizes the fixture hazard already recorded three times: a mutation escaping means the suite never enters the state the guard defends, and tightening a guard is exactly when that state stops being reachable by the existing tests.
PORT-OWNED SENTINELS. Renamed too: the predicate is "not a terminal failure without a result", not "produced a real answer", which was true of neither branch.
A MUTATION THAT DID NOT RUN, for the second time in this programme. Slice 6a, comparison persistence and rehydration: complete. The plan called slice 6 "the HTTP surface", but the endpoints need somewhere to put a comparison and something that can get one back, and corpus persistence was its own slice for the same reason. Split: 6a is the store and the rehydration, 6b is POST /v1/comparisons and GET /v1/comparisons/{comparisonID}.
THE DESIGN PROBLEM THIS SLICE ACTUALLY FOUND, and it was not visible from the plan: a stored comparison CANNOT be rebuilt through its own constructor. NewComparisonPolicy takes authored CheckpointKeys and two compiled Plans; ComparisonPolicy retains only the derived CheckpointCorrespondences, and a CheckpointID is a digest of (plan, key), so the keys are gone. Storing the policy value is impossible and storing the keys separately would let a caller supply keys belonging to a different policy — accepted at write time, surfacing much later as somebody else's integrity error.
Resolved by storing only the DERIVED correspondences and recovering the keys at rehydration: load both plans, derive the identity of every checkpoint each one declares, and find which declaration produces each stored identity. That is a search over declarations, not an inversion, so the kernel's one-way rule is intact — and it means every stored field is a component of the identity the row is filed under. Nothing is stored beside the name that the name does not already commit to.
THE PORT IS ASYMMETRIC ON PURPOSE. PutComparison takes an authenticated semantic.Comparison; GetComparison returns a ports.ComparisonRecord, which is documented as a projection carrying no authority. Taking a record on the way in would let a caller assemble one whose parts disagree. The asymmetry IS the statement that a store can hold a description of a comparison and can never hold a comparison.
WHY THE AUTHENTICATION IS IN app AND NOT THE ADAPTER, unlike corpora: a Corpus rebuilds from its own document, so the corpus adapter can re-derive the identity itself. A comparison's policy needs two compiled PLANS, and a ComparisonStore implementation is not guaranteed to have plan access — PlanStore and ComparisonStore are separate interfaces. The port would be unimplementable by an adapter that only knows comparisons. So RehydrateComparison lives in app, which has both stores, and the adapter honestly returns a projection.
A KERNEL ADDITION THAT WAS OVERDUE: Plan.CheckpointID(key) (CheckpointID, bool). Before this, the only way to obtain a CheckpointID outside the kernel was to EXECUTE the plan and read a sealed checkpoint — which is what two existing test helpers actually did. Forward derivation only; no decoder. The bool is the load-bearing part: an identity can be derived for any (plan, key) pair including one the plan never declares, and such an identity is perfectly well formed while naming a checkpoint nothing can realize.
