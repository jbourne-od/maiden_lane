# Replay corpus and comparison — progress

Programme plan: docs/superpowers/plans/2026-08-20-replay-corpus-and-comparison.md

DECOMPOSITION FINDING that made this programme tractable, and it came from reading clauses 6 and 7 apart rather than as one thing. Clause 6 is a COMPARABILITY PRECONDITION: did the two sides run over the same corpus, under the same profile, in the same world, with an explicit correspondence between the checkpoint semantics compared. Clause 7 is the OUTCOME: no protected metric regression. They are usually thought of together as "did the change make things worse", and separating them means this programme can deliver clause 6 without inventing a metric system. A comparison whose comparability cannot be established is worthless whatever the metrics show, which is why it is first.
DECISION 1: a corpus is CONTENT-ADDRESSED, not a named collection. The reason is clause 6's own wording — it requires the two sides to have run over THE SAME replay corpus. Under a mutable name, sameness would be a claim about a label: two sides could satisfy the clause having run over different sets, and every comparison ever made against that name would retroactively be about something else. Content-addressing also gives the right failure, since adding a case changes CorpusID and a comparison over the enlarged corpus is visibly a different comparison rather than the old one with better numbers.
This DIFFERS from the target-policy decision in the previous programme, and the difference is in the specification rather than in taste. §14.1 says a publication record pins the policy VERSION, explicitly choosing a version over a derived identity. §14.2 says the corpus participates in a derived IDENTITY. Both calls were made by the spec; this programme only follows them.
DECISION 2, settled by a fact rather than a preference: InputID = H(StateDigest, WorldID), and §14.2 lists WorldID and CorpusID as SEPARATE inputs to Compare. Both point the same way, so a corpus is a set of case STATES and the world is pinned once for the whole comparison. CorpusID = H(ordered StateDigests). Together corpus and world determine every case's InputID, which is what makes "the same corpus under the same historical world" checkable rather than two labels that happen to match. §14.2's own phrase, "historical world/corpus", is one concept and this is what it decomposes into.
DECISION 3: comparison is a derived semantic artifact of the same kind as an assessment. §6 enumerates every derived identity and neither CorpusID nor ComparisonID appears — but unlike the target-policy case that absence is NOT decisive here, because §14.2 says in as many words that the inputs "participate in comparison identity". The spec asserts the identity exists; §6 simply does not enumerate it. The shape follows AssessmentID = H(CheckpointArtifactID, ProfileID), the existing precedent for a derived judgment about artifacts.
The two sides of a comparison are CheckpointIDs, not CheckpointArtifactIDs, and that is the crux. CheckpointID = H(PlanID, CheckpointKey) identifies a checkpoint DECLARATION — a piece of semantics — while CheckpointArtifactID identifies one realized checkpoint of one run. A comparison over a corpus has no single artifact per side; it has one per case. So what is compared is the semantics evaluated across the corpus, which is exactly §14.2's phrase "corresponding checkpoint semantics".
DECISION 4: correspondence is DECLARED, never inferred. Inference by name would be right most of the time, and the times it was wrong would be indistinguishable from the times it was right — two plans may legitimately name the same semantics differently and, far worse, may name different semantics the same.
COST STATED RATHER THAN DISCOVERED: a corpus of n cases needs n executions per side. It is affordable only because execution is deterministic and identity derived, so a case already executed under a given ExecutionID never needs executing again — and since the rehydration slice, the stored result is verifiably reproducible. A corpus costs n executions once; comparisons over it afterwards cost lookups.

Slice 1, corpus identity in the kernel: complete. This is the programme's first kernel change and it follows World exactly — validate, canonically order, refuse duplicates, domain-tagged versioned encoding, encoders and no decoders.
Corpus holds States rather than state digests. Accepting digests would let a CorpusID be derived over content nobody has; every digest here is one the kernel computed from a state it validated. It also makes the value useful, since running a side over a corpus needs the states and a corpus of hashes could only be compared, never replayed.
An empty corpus is REFUSED, unlike an empty world, and the asymmetry is deliberate: an empty world is a real statement — nothing was pinned — while a comparison over no cases establishes nothing about a candidate. Admitting one would let clause 6 be satisfied by being vacuously true, which is the most dangerous way for a gate to pass. A test asserts both halves so the asymmetry is anchored rather than described.
A duplicate case is refused rather than deduplicated. Silently collapsing it would answer a different question than the one asked while looking like it answered this one: a comparison reported over twelve cases that ran eleven is a false statement about coverage.
Case is indexed rather than returning the whole set, because copying every state to reach one would make iterating a corpus quadratic in its own size — a real concern at the hundreds of cases a corpus is expected to hold, and not one World has.
InputIdentities is asserted against BindRun's own derivation rather than against a recomputation of the same formula in the test. A corpus that derived inputs no execution could match would be useless while looking correct.
MUTATION TESTING, and the first attempt at it was worthless: removing the ordering and removing the duplicate check both produced BUILD failures from unused variables, so the tests never ran and proved nothing. Redone with mutations that compile — a no-op stable sort, and a condition short-circuited while keeping the loop index used — and all three properties then failed by name: order independence, duplicate refusal, and input-identity agreement. Worth recording as a method note: a mutation that does not compile is not a mutation test.

OWNER REVIEW of #26: both identity decisions RATIFIED, two changes requested, both accepted.
Ratified explicitly, with reasons worth keeping: CorpusID is content-addressed despite §6 not listing it, because treating §6's list as eternally exhaustive would require ignoring the more specific section that introduced the concept later. And CheckpointID rather than CheckpointArtifactID is the comparison-side identity, because a corpus comparison asks a semantic question — how does declaration A behave versus declaration B over this corpus, world, and profile — and there is no singular artifact per side, only n realizations.
FORWARD CONSTRAINT SET BY THE OWNER, recorded in the plan so slice 2 cannot drift into it: ComparisonID identifies the QUESTION; the per-case ExecutionIDs and CheckpointArtifactIDs are the EVIDENCE that answered it. Do not fold the evidence into the identity for auditability. That would collapse question and evidence — the same category error as storing a gate verdict beside the artifacts it summarizes — and would make a comparison's identity change every time it was re-evidenced.
CHANGE 1, and it was a real contradiction in my own plan: I wrote that a corpus costs n executions once and later comparisons cost lookups, then in the next section said comparison must consume authenticated artifacts. Those cannot both be true, because #24's Rehydrate authenticates by RE-EXECUTING. A later comparison over persisted history therefore costs roughly 2n re-executions, not 2n once followed by lookups.
The correction separates three things that collapsing produces the wrong answer from: EXECUTION REUSE, where a derived ExecutionID means an already-executed case is never enqueued or stored twice and which is what makes a corpus accumulate rather than repeat; AUTHENTICATION COST, which is a re-execution today and is the price of refusing to let a stored projection carry authorization weight rather than a missing optimization; and ANY FUTURE CACHE, which needs its own trust argument and may not promote the stored projection to authority — the already-rejected option does not become correct by being called a cache.
The owner's framing of why this mattered is the part to keep: it is the kind of apparently minor wording error that becomes an architectural assumption three slices later, when a slice "optimizes" comparison by reading stored results and reintroduces the trust model this system spent three slices removing.
CHANGE 2: Corpus{} reintroduced the empty-corpus case through InputIdentities. NewCorpus refuses zero cases specifically so clause 6 cannot be satisfied by being vacuously true, and the zero value walked back into exactly that state through the one method that asserts what inputs exist — it iterated nothing and returned nil error. The constructor's refusal has to hold there or it is a suggestion about how to build one rather than a refusal.
Closed with an established() check InputIdentities calls, deliberately a method rather than an inline test because the comparison contract needs the same question answered and a second implementation of "is this a real corpus" is a second chance to answer it differently. Verified by deletion: the new test fails by name.
The other accessors are deliberately left alone, on the owner's reasoning: ID() == "", Len() == 0 and Case() reporting absence are harmless descriptions of an empty value. The method that ASSERTS semantic input identity is the one that must not turn invalid absence into successful emptiness. A test pins that distinction so a later change making them error is a deliberate decision rather than a side effect.

Slice 2, the comparison contract: complete. ComparisonPolicy with an explicit checkpoint correspondence, ComparisonPolicyID, and ComparisonID over §14.2's full formula.
Correspondence is authored as checkpoint KEYS and derived into identities against the two plans. Keys are the authoring form — somebody writing a correspondence knows that team_hos_aggregated.v1 is now team_hos_reconciled.v2 — and deriving against the plans means a pair naming a checkpoint neither plan declares cannot become a policy. A correspondence therefore cannot outlive the declarations it describes.
The one-to-one requirement in BOTH directions is the fail-closed property that matters most after declaration itself. One baseline mapping to two candidates, or two baselines to one candidate, makes "corresponding" ambiguous, and a comparison cannot fail closed on an ambiguity it silently resolved by taking the first match.
Corresponds checks BOTH sides rather than resolving the baseline and trusting the result. Checking only that the baseline maps to something would accept a comparison whose candidate side is a checkpoint the contract never mentioned. Mutation-tested: weakening it to a one-sided lookup is caught.
Failing closed happens at CONSTRUCTION, not at evaluation. NewComparison refuses when the policy declares no correspondence between its two sides, so an unidentifiable comparison cannot be handed to downstream code that might not remember to check. Checking later would make the refusal a behaviour somebody has to remember rather than a state that cannot be built.
The owner's forward constraint is honoured and documented on the type: a Comparison names the QUESTION. Per-case ExecutionIDs and CheckpointArtifactIDs are evidence and are deliberately absent from the identity, because folding them in would collapse question and evidence and make the identity change every time the comparison was re-evidenced — so the same question asked twice would be two questions. A test asserts the same request produces the same identity.
Checkpoint identities are asserted against SEAL's own derivation rather than a recomputation of the same formula, the same pattern as slice 1's InputIdentities-versus-BindRun check. A policy mapping checkpoints no sealed artifact ever names would otherwise look correct.
MUTATION TESTING FOUND A TEST THAT PASSED FOR THE WRONG REASON, which is the second time this method has earned its place in this programme. Five properties were mutated; four were caught. The escape: dropping the correspondence digests from the policy's canonical encoding, keeping only the count, was NOT detected — because the identity test varied the policy by supplying a NARROWER one, which differs in count. So the test proved the count participates, not the content.
The consequence would have been exactly the failure this identity exists to prevent: two policies over the same plans, with the same number of rows and different pairings, sharing one identity — letting a comparison be reinterpreted under a correspondence nobody declared. Closed with a test using two policies that are both valid, both one-to-one, both two rows, and mean completely different things, and verified by re-running the mutation.
METHOD NOTE, now twice-learned in this programme: a passing test is not evidence that the property it names is enforced. In slice 1 the failure mode was mutations that did not compile; here it was a fixture that varied the wrong dimension. Both were invisible from a green run.

Slice 3, corpus persistence: complete. ports.CorpusStore, both adapters, one contract suite, and a migration. store-check 79 to 97.
A corpus stores like a PLAN, not like a policy, and the plan document's word "append-only" was imprecise. Identity is content, so a corpus cannot be edited at all: PutCorpus is idempotent on CorpusID and there is no version, no update, and no delete. A corpus that should have held different cases IS a different corpus, and the one already stored must remain readable because comparisons pin its identity — deleting it would leave them naming a set of cases nothing can reconstruct.
KERNEL REFINEMENT FOUND BY WORKING THIS SLICE: a corpus must share one schema across every case. Storage wanted to keep the schema once, and the reason it can is stronger than convenience — BindRun refuses a state whose schema digest is not the plan's, so a mixed-schema corpus could never be replayed under ANY plan. It is unusable for its only purpose, and refusing it at assembly means that is discovered when the corpus is built rather than partway through executing it. Corpus.SchemaDigest() is exposed, and comparability will need it: both plans under comparison must pin that digest or the corpus is not replayable under both.
The schema is deliberately NOT part of CorpusID. Every case's StateDigest already commits to it, so encoding it would be a second statement of one fact.
Storage follows the ratified rehydration pattern rather than inventing one: a Corpus cannot be serialized, so the adapter stores the cases as documents, rebuilds them through the kernel's constructors on read, and REQUIRES the re-derived CorpusID to equal the one the row is filed under. That is the same rule plan storage follows when it recompiles on read, and it is what makes a stored corpus verifiable rather than merely well formed.
The projected columns — schema_digest and case_count — are checked against the rebuilt corpus rather than trusted. They exist so a reader can size work without decoding, and a column that disagreed with the cases would be a second description of them able to mislead.
The corruption test's most valuable case is a document that decodes cleanly, rebuilds into a perfectly valid corpus, and is a DIFFERENT corpus. Nothing about that row looks wrong; only the re-derived identity catches it.
FINDING WHILE WRITING THAT TEST: the "syntactically unreadable document" case is unrepresentable in this table, because the column is jsonb and PostgreSQL rejects malformed JSON on write — the UPDATE itself failed. The plan and execution tables store bytea and do test it. That difference is correct rather than an inconsistency: the column type is doing work here that application code has to do there. Replaced with a document that is valid JSON and describes no corpus, which the empty-corpus refusal then rejects, and the reason is recorded in the test so nobody adds the impossible case back.
